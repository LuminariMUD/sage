# Myths of the Glassborn

## 1. The Miner's Tale (Common among dwarves and human miners)

In the deep taverns where pickmen drink, the story of the Glassborn is told as a warning.

They say the clan that fled below grew hard and cold, turning to crystal when the world above burned. Their bodies were sharp as shattered glass, and if struck, their skin could cut deeper than any axe. Their hearts were blazing lanterns that could blind a man if he looked too long.

Miners whisper that the Glassborn still prowl the deepest shafts, jealous of anyone who dares to cut crystal from the stone. When a miner is crushed in a collapse or lost to the dark, it's said the Glassborn struck him down for stealing what is rightfully theirs.

*(Truths: crystalline bodies, glowing hearts. Distortions: sharp like broken glass, vengeance against miners.)*

---

## 2. The Sailor's Song (Common along coasts, where crystal from the mountains was traded)

Among sailors, the Glassborn were never creatures of stone at all, but children of the sea. They believed the dwarves who fled underground dug so far they found the ocean's heart — and the pressure and salt turned them into crystal.

They say the Glassborn glow beneath the waves like lanternfish, their voices ringing like bells through the water. Sailors swore that if you pressed a shell to your ear, you could hear their choirs echoing beneath the tide. Some claimed they swam beside ships on moonless nights, guiding or cursing sailors depending on whether the crew carried crystal in their cargo.

*(Truths: harmonic speech. Distortions: aquatic nature, guiding ships like will-o'-wisps.)*

---

## 3. The Priest's Parable (Told by clergy, moralizing version)

Priests told of a dwarven clan so greedy for crystal that they forsook the gods to hoard it. For this arrogance, they were punished: their flesh was turned to the very stone they loved, their hearts trapped as flickering candles within their bodies.

According to the priests, the Glassborn live eternally, unable to eat, drink, or rest, cursed to admire their own shining forms for all time. They are described as vain, parading their jeweled bodies, gilded with molten gold to hide their flaws. Children were warned: covet not what shines too brightly, lest you become as hollow as the Glassborn.

*(Truths: loss of eating/drinking, gilding custom, hearts of light. Distortions: vanity as a curse, divine punishment instead of evolution.)*

---

Each version contains kernels of truth that the rediscovered Crystal Dwarves later confirmed — glowing hearts, harmonic voices, gilded bodies.

But each is colored by the teller's worldview:

Miners feared them as jealous spirits.

Sailors romanticized them as mystical guides.

Priests cast them as a cautionary tale of greed.

---

## After the Rediscovery: The Glassborn Made Flesh

### 1. The Miner's Tale Revised

When the gates of Nagburim opened, miners everywhere muttered that their grandfathers had been right. The "Glassborn" were no ghost stories after all.

True, the Crystal Dwarves were no vengeful wraiths haunting shafts — but they were born of crystal, and their hearts glowed just as the tales said. And when word spread that the dwarves guarded their Arcanite veins jealously and struck down trespassers without mercy, the old warnings took on new teeth.

Now, miners tell their sons: "Leave the crystal veins be. They're not just stone — they're the blood of the Glassborn. And they'll come for you if you steal it."

*(The myth survives, reshaped into respect and fear of the dwarves' monopoly.)*

---

### 2. The Sailor's Song Revised

When sailors first saw the Crystal Dwarves in the flesh, they laughed that the old songs had been only half-wrong. The dwarves did not swim the seas — but their voices did ring like choirs, each word a harmony of bells and flutes. To a sailor's ear, it was music enough to stir memory of the myths.

So the songs were changed. Now it is said that the Glassborn do not swim beneath the waves, but their music travels far through stone, echoing under mountains and rivers until it reaches the sea. When a sailor presses a shell to his ear and hears the deep hum within, he claims it is the echo of Nagburim's choirs, still carried in the bones of the world.

*(A poetic reframing — they didn't find the sea, but their voices still reach it.)*

---

### 3. The Priest's Parable Revised

The priests, confronted with living Crystal Dwarves, softened their sermons. They could no longer preach that the Glassborn were cursed for greed — for here they stood, noble and terrible, not damned but exalted.

So the story changed: the dwarves who delved into crystal were not punished, but tested. Those who survived the trial were remade into something closer to perfection, their impurities burned away. Gilding, once mocked as vanity, was reframed as penance: a way for the flawed to humble themselves until their spirits were refined.

Now, priests still tell the tale of the Glassborn, but as a warning against arrogance and a parable of transformation: "As crystal is forged in fire, so too may imperfection be made pure."

*(A myth turned moral lesson of endurance and spiritual growth.)*

---

## In the Common Tongue

To most folk, the word Glassborn is still used in taverns and stories, though the Crystal Dwarves themselves reject it. To them, it is a name of ignorance, tied to a time when the world thought them fairy tales.

But among surface dwellers, the myths live on, reshaped into truths half-recognized:

Miners still mutter their warnings.

Sailors still hum their songs.

Priests still preach their parables.

Only now, when they speak of the Glassborn, they do so with a nervous glance toward the east, toward Nagburim — where myths walk in flesh and crystal.
