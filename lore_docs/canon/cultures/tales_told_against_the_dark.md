## A Necessary Warning About Stories

The tales in this concordance are canonically told in Lumia.

That does not make every event inside them canonically true.

This distinction is the hinge on which folklore turns. A miner's warning may
preserve a Crystal Dwarf's glowing heart and invent a murder in the same breath.
A temple parable may remember an ancient sacrifice while giving its own god the
best line. A child's rhyme may carry a road-name more accurately than the royal
survey and everything else backward.

Stories in Lumia possess unusual weight because the Loom carries Memory, Will,
and Possibility. Repetition can shape expectation. Expectation can shape choice.
Choice can make an old tale appear prophetic without changing one stone of the
past.

Some bards insist stories can do more.

They tell excellent stories about it.

## The First Lie

Across many peoples survives a tale called **How the World Learned to Lie**.
Every tradition claims to have told it first.

In the beginning, says the tale, the world could not distinguish speech from
fact. To say _hunger_ created an empty belly. To name an enemy began a war. To
promise tomorrow forced the sun to rise whether the sky was ready or not.

Then came the Luminari, who had learned to speak of what was not yet true.

They gave the world three merciful lies:

- “We are separate,” and selves acquired boundaries;
- “The deed is gone,” and consequence learned to follow time;
- “It might be otherwise,” and possibility opened its first door.

One among them spoke a fourth: “What is separate can be made whole.” The others
heard a threat inside the mercy. The speaker heard cruelty inside their fear.
Thus began the oldest argument and, according to certain tavern versions, the
second-oldest lawsuit.

Secret historians recognize the shadow of the Shattered Truth in this story.
They do not accept its words as a literal creation account. The Lock of Identity
is not a sentence carved on a pillar, and causality was not invented by a clever
turn of phrase.

Still, people whisper the three lies when a Darkling tries to make unity sound
like peace.

## The Fire That Escaped

### The Dragon Telling

Dragons say fire was never stolen. It was possibility before possibility learned
to remain inside scale, coal, star, or hearth.

Pyrythis the Unbound refused that lesson. She swallowed the Luminari teaching
called Restraint and breathed until half the horizon forgot it had ever been
dark. This is why every flame leans outward. It remembers being larger.

### The Human Telling

Humans name the thief Dozen-Fingers, who stole not flame but the method of making
it. A Titan cursed the knowledge with appetite, ensuring every fire would demand
fuel as Dozen-Fingers had demanded power.

The tale explains why human ambition is praised in workshops and blamed after
wars.

### The Embassy Telling

Primal callers offer a stranger account: each flame is a narrow embassy of the
Primal Reach. Fuel grants it temporary ground. A hearth is a treaty. A wildfire
is a border dispute in which weather has armed one side.

No version is settled history. All three are taught to apprentices, because a
caster who thinks fire is only heat has not yet noticed which way it is looking.

## Three Tales of the Glassborn

Before Nagburim opened, surface peoples called its hidden inhabitants the
**Glassborn**. Crystal Dwarves generally reject the name. It belongs to centuries
in which strangers made them into warning, wonder, and punishment while they
could not answer.

### The Miner's Warning

Miners said a clan fled downward during the Last War and drank light from
stone-stars. Their skin sharpened to glass. Their hearts became lanterns. They
guarded deep veins so jealously that every unexplained collapse was charged to
their account.

The warning ended: _Strike no singing crystal before you ask who is listening._

After the gates opened, the tale changed very little. The glowing hearts proved
true. The murderous ghosts proved to be a people with mining law, soldiers, and
excellent reasons to distrust unauthorized extraction. Surface miners found
this close enough for their grandfathers to remain correct.

### The Sailor's Song

Sailors claimed the hidden dwarves dug through the root of the mountains into
the sea's heart, where pressure and salt made crystal of them. Their bell-voices
traveled beneath the water. A shell held to the ear carried a distant Nagburim
choir.

The Crystal Dwarves were not aquatic when they emerged. Sailors revised the
song: Tal traveled through stone beneath the sea instead. A good chorus is more
resistant to evidence than a bad map.

### The Priest's Lesson

Certain priests preached that greedy dwarves coveted Arcanite until they became
the object of their desire—beautiful, hollow, and unable to eat. Gilding was
vanity. Transparency was punishment made visible.

Living Crystal Dwarves made the sermon diplomatically expensive. Modern
versions call the transformation a trial and gilding an emblem of endurance.
Nagburim theologians note that the moral changed precisely when the supposed
object lesson acquired ambassadors.

### What the Tales Kept

All three traditions preserved fragments: crystalline bodies, luminous hearts,
harmonic speech, the end of ordinary hunger, and gilded fractures. Each placed
those facts inside the teller's own fear.

This is why the Glassborn cycle is taught in historical method. A story can be
wrong in every conclusion and still carry the only surviving evidence.

## The Dream of the First Forest

Elven households tell that the Otherworld once dreamed a forest before tree,
soil, or season existed. The dream wanted to know whether it could become real
without ceasing to be dream. Elves were its first attempt to answer.

In the oldest telling, each elven refuge keeps a **Dream Seed**, a memory of the
forest-that-was-not. As long as the seed is remembered, the refuge stands with
one root in the Otherworld. When the final keeper forgets, every door begins
opening onto the same ordinary morning.

The story likely preserves cultural memory of the Pools of Twilight and the
Feyveil. No canon locates the Pools. No dependable survey has found a universal
Dream Seed beneath every elven settlement.

Elves continue checking.

## The Hundred Heroes

Humans say they began with no proper heroes and therefore borrowed a hundred.
One stole courage from an orcish death-song. One stole an elf's memory. One
stole endurance from a dwarven bridge. One stole purpose from an Arcana Golem
and spent the rest of his life trying to give it back.

Each theft became a failure. Courage without judgment became folly. Memory
without mercy became paralysis. Endurance became refusal to leave a lost cause.
Purpose became a cage with the door painted open.

The hundred failures produced the first human gift no elder people could claim:
the habit of trying again without pretending the previous attempt had secretly
succeeded.

Most regions can name between twelve and two hundred of the Hundred. Ashenport
maintains that numerical accuracy would spoil the point.

## What Will You Fight For?

The best-known half-orc origin tale begins during the First Darkling Wars.

Rage said, “We need a weapon that can feel.”

Endurance answered, “We need a feeling that can choose.”

Together they took the fury of existence refusing to end and gave it flesh. To
each new warrior they asked one question: _What will you fight for?_

Those who named a cause became heroes. Those who answered _everything_ became
monsters. Those who answered _nothing_ became philosophers and were watched more
carefully than either.

Some versions name Rage and Endurance **Cassis** and **Keridur**. The canonical
divine concordance does not recognize those names among the Forty Woven Gods.
They may be saints, old titles, Chulani principles given voices, or deities whose
anchors did not survive the war.

Historical accounts instead credit Chulani sorcerers working for the Anterian
Alliance with creating the first half-orc soldiers. Many half-orcs hold both
accounts: Chulan made the bodies; the question made a people.

At adulthood, some communities still observe a **Choosing**. The participant
names what they will defend, what they will never defend, and who may tell them
the difference when rage makes both look alike. The declaration can be changed.
It should be. A purpose that cannot survive revision is only another master.

## Places That Tell Stories Differently

### Ashenport: Borrowed Tales

At the Jade Jug and a dozen less reputable rooms, a drink may be bought with a
story. The most famous version of the custom claims that any tale told to the
nameless keeper leaves the teller's memory forever.

Regular patrons say this is nonsense. They also become evasive when asked how
the keeper knows childhood incidents they have forgotten.

During the **Ashenstory**, contestants tell plausible lies before ward judges.
The winning lie is reenacted for one day as though it were civic fact. The year
someone declared taxes optional is remembered fondly by everyone except the
archive, which has misplaced the festival record.

### Mosswood: The Green Telling

Mosswood stories rarely begin with “once.” They begin with weather, leaf-fall,
animal movement, and whatever the oldest root has pushed to the surface.

Each spring, interpreters offer a **Green Telling**: one account of what the
forest appears to expect before winter. It is not binding prophecy. The forest
votes, argues, changes its mind, and occasionally eats the evidence.

### The Crystal Reaches: Proof-Songs

Nagburim preserves certain epics as harmonic proofs. Motifs return under altered
conditions; contradictions sound as unresolved chords; a conclusion is true
only if the full performance bears its weight.

Surface storytellers joke that the **Ballad of Infinite Sums** has been underway
for three centuries and has not reached the second tragedy. Resonators reply
that surface stories rush their conclusions and therefore require sequels.

## Wandering Story-Patterns

Some tales behave like quests seeking new actors. Whether this is magic, curse,
artifact influence, or the human talent for repeating mistakes remains
unsettled.

### The Liberator's Burden

Those who free a captive sometimes become unable to pass any locked thing
without wondering whether it suffers. The concern begins nobly and widens:
prisoners, animals, sealed books, plague houses, old gods, the Prisoner.

The burden ends, storytellers say, only when the liberator learns that mercy may
guard a door as well as open it.

### The Dragon's Auditor

Anyone who counts ten thousand things risks dreaming of a dragon's uncatalogued
hoard. In the dream, the dragon welcomes the accountant. A hoard accurately
remembered is harder to steal.

The true task is to find the single object that does not belong. Every dragon
possesses one. Every empire does too.

### The Darkling Mirror

A person who kills a Darkling may begin seeing a darkened reflection performing
the cruelties the killer believes victory made impossible. The reflection grows
stronger whenever denied and weaker whenever questioned honestly.

The crude ending is to break the mirror. The better ending is to ask which act
belongs to corruption and which was already waiting in the hero.

## Forbidden Tales

### The Last Story

A tale beginning _When the final word is spoken_ is said to end every other
story when completed. No complete text exists. Ruins across Lumia contain
different second sentences, each in a voice that falls silent before the third.

### The Prisoner's Testament

Darkling circles speak of a record in which the Prisoner is not villain but the
only Luminari willing to heal a shattered whole. Secret canon confirms the
philosophical possibility. It does not confirm that a journal, five-volume set,
or physical text exists.

Anyone selling “the missing volume beside the Fifth Lock” is trafficking in
false geography, dangerous sympathy, or both.

### The Name That Wants a Dominion

The Twenty-First Titan is a Woven possibility attempting to become a Deep
necessity. Stories that provide it a face, appetite, and childhood may help it
acquire the identity it lacks.

For this reason, responsible tellers preserve the warning and omit the supposed
name. Irresponsible tellers attract excellent audiences for a regrettably short
time.

## Bardic Resonance

Bards do not command reality merely by describing a preferred ending. If this
were possible, every siege would be settled by competing choruses and every
chorus by the wealthy patron with more tenors.

A practiced performance can, however, align memory, will, and possibility among
its witnesses. A victory song does not fetch victory from the future. It helps a
terrified company inhabit the version of itself capable of holding another
minute. A lament does not create grief from nothing. It gives grief a shape that
can be shared without becoming identical.

At rare sites, before prepared witnesses, or through a storied relic, this
resonance may become visibly magical. Words acquire force. Repeated action finds
an easier path. A public identity can split from the private person until the
legend seems to walk alone.

Such workings obey three cautions:

- no story creates consequence without actors paying it;
- an audience is participant, not empty material;
- the simpler the legend becomes, the more of the living person it excludes.

Heroes sometimes spend their later lives fighting the perfect, merciless
versions of themselves that songs left behind.

## Provenance and Promotion Decisions

This document rewrites the durable folklore in the retired
`lore_docs/drafts/world/TALES_AND_LEGENDS.md` and reconciles it with the
canonical history, Locks, divine concordance, racial origins, Homelands, and
relic system.

The First Lie, fire traditions, Glassborn cycle, First Forest, Hundred Heroes,
half-orc Choosing, regional storytelling, wandering patterns, forbidden tales,
and bardic resonance are retained as established traditions. Claims that every
story automatically becomes reality, that the Five Locks conceal physical
journal volumes, that Cassis and Keridur are unambiguously canonical deities, or
that performance can select outcomes without cost are retired or reframed as
unreliable belief.
